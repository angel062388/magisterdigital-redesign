02 · The position most operators are in

Most competitors, not running ads at all

If you sell garage floors, storage, or cabinets, most of your local competitors are not advertising on Meta at all. Of the 42 companies we audited across five markets, 26 ran no Meta ads.

The ones who do advertise tend to run the same national template, a pain hook, a dollars-off offer, and a lifetime warranty, into a local auction that does not resemble the one two states over. That is the position most operators are in when they decide whether Meta is worth it: guessing at who they are bidding against, and worried the answer from an agency will be a generic package built on national best practices.

The owners we built this for had usually been sold copy before, three headlines, a stock photo of a clean garage, a lead form, and a cost-per-lead report, and wanted to know what actually runs in their market before spending into it.

The method · five steps before anyone writes an ad

We map the market before we write the copy

This is the part most agencies skip, because it is slow and produces nothing billable on day one.

01Find every advertiser, not the obvious ones

Franchise location pages, local independents, and national e-commerce brands each behave differently, so each is pulled separately. In Southern California that meant checking 22 companies across five counties to establish that only three were running ads.

02Read the winners without spend data

The US Ads Library hides impressions and spend for commercial advertisers, so we use the two signals it does expose: how long an ad has run continuously, and how many near-identical variants are live. Both cost the advertiser money, so both signal confidence.

03Transcribe the copy verbatim, ad by ad

Offers, price framing, warranty language, install-speed claims, and proof stacks are recorded as written, not paraphrased. About 300 ads went through this. It is the slow part, and it is the part that produces everything useful.

04Treat each market as its own problem

The single largest finding in the study is that the five markets barely resemble each other. A strategy built for one of them can be actively wrong in another.

05Separate evidence from what we would like to believe

Longevity proves commitment, not profit. A heavily duplicated ad proves confidence, not lead quality. Those boundaries get flagged, not dressed up as fact.

05 What survives: six ingredients Strip every long-running ad down and the same parts remain. Not a template; a residue. OpenClose
What survives · six ingredients

Strip every long-running ad down and the same parts remain

This is not an invented template. It is what is left when the study’s longest-running ads are reduced to their shared components.

01A hook that names the pain

The opening line describes the garage the homeowner is embarrassed by: clutter, cracks, stains, a car stuck in the driveway.

02A hard number

A price, a range, or a dollars-off figure. Every ad in the study still running past six months contained one.

03Speed of transformation

“1 day” or “2 days” appears in nearly every long-running flooring ad read.

04Warranty as risk reversal

Lifetime coverage is effectively table stakes among franchise advertisers. Independents that omit it compete without a net.

05Quantified proof

Counts, not adjectives: reviews, floors installed, garages transformed, years in business, local awards.

06An identity marker

Who the owner is. It closes the trust gap created by letting a stranger install something permanent in your home.

Across all five markets, every ad still running after six months named a price, a count, a warranty term, or a timeframe. Not one long-running winner relied on adjectives alone.

  • 03Our own numbers stop at the lead. The eight campaigns show lead volume and cost per lead over a trailing, partial delivery window as captured in the account. They do not establish booked jobs, close rate, or revenue.
  • 04The accounts belong to the client. The campaigns and data shown sit on ad accounts the client owns. The client is anonymized here; competitor names are drawn from the public library.
